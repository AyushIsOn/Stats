WS-1 · Mines Heat Capacity — F-test for equal variances repeats 3×

Data: Mine 1: 8260, 8130, 8350, 8070, 8340 (n₁=5)  |  Mine 2: 7950, 7890, 7900, 8140, 7920, 7840 (n₂=6)

Step 1 — Hypotheses: $H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$   vs   $H_1: \sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2$

Step 2 — α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Step 3 — Sample variances: $\bar{x}_1 = 8230,\ s_1^2 = 15750$;   $\bar{x}_2 = 7940,\ s_2^2 = 10920$.

Step 4 — Test statistic (larger variance on top): $F = \dfrac{15750}{10920} = 1.442$,   df = (4, 5).

Step 5 — Critical value: $F_{0.05,\,4,5} = 5.19$. Since $1.442 < 5.19$, do NOT reject $H_0$.

Step 6 — Conclusion: The two population variances can be considered equal; no significant difference in variability.

WS-2 · Heights of 10 Adult Males — one-sample t-test repeats 2×

Data: n = 10, $\bar{x}$ = 158 cm, variance = 39.0625 (so s = 6.25). Claim: average height < 162.5 cm.

Step 1: $H_0: \mu = 162.5$   vs   $H_1: \mu < 162.5$ (one-tailed, left).

Step 2 — α = 0.05.

Step 3 — Test statistic: $t = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{158 - 162.5}{6.25/\sqrt{10}} = \dfrac{-4.5}{1.976} = -2.277$,   df = 9.

Step 4 — Critical value: $-t_{0.05,\,9} = -1.833$.

Step 5 — Decision: $-2.277 < -1.833$, so it falls in the rejection region → reject $H_0$.

Step 6 — Conclusion: Adult males in the locality are, on average, significantly shorter than 162.5 cm.

WS-3 · Pesticide Bags — one-sample t-test repeats 2×

Data: 50, 49, 44, 52, 45, 48, 46, 45, 49, 45 (n = 10). Test if average packing = 50 kg.

Step 1: $H_0: \mu = 50$   vs   $H_1: \mu \neq 50$ (two-tailed).

Step 2 — α = 0.05.

Step 3 — Sample stats: $\bar{x} = 47.3$,   $s = 2.669$.

Test statistic: $t = \dfrac{47.3 - 50}{2.669/\sqrt{10}} = \dfrac{-2.7}{0.844} = -3.199$,   df = 9.

Step 4 — Critical value: $\pm t_{0.025,\,9} = \pm 2.262$.

Step 5 — Decision: $|-3.199| > 2.262$ → reject $H_0$.

Step 6 — Conclusion: The average packing is significantly different from 50 kg (it is less).

Key Concepts & Theory

Basic Definitions

Random Experiment
An experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty (e.g., tossing a coin, rolling a die).
Sample Space (S)
The set of all possible outcomes. E.g., rolling a die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
Event
A subset of the sample space. E.g., getting an even number A = {2, 4, 6}.
Classical Probability
$P(E) = \dfrac{\text{No. of favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total no. of outcomes}} = \dfrac{n(E)}{n(S)}$

Axioms of Probability (Kolmogorov)

  • $0 \leq P(A) \leq 1$ for any event A
  • $P(S) = 1$ (certainty), $P(\phi) = 0$ (impossible)
  • For mutually exclusive events: $P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \dots) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) + \dots$
  • Complement: $P(A) + P(A^c) = 1$

Addition Rule

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

If A and B are mutually exclusive ($A \cap B = \phi$): $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$

For three events:

$$P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A)+P(B)+P(C) - P(A\cap B) - P(B\cap C) - P(A\cap C) + P(A\cap B\cap C)$$

Example: A card is drawn from a deck. P(spade or ace) = $\frac{13}{52} + \frac{4}{52} - \frac{1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13}$.

Conditional Probability & Multiplication Theorem

$$P(B|A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}, \quad P(A) > 0$$

Rearranging gives the multiplication theorem:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A) = P(B) \cdot P(A|B)$$

Worked example (from material): A flight departs on time P(D)=0.83, arrives on time P(A)=0.82, both P(D∩A)=0.78.

  • P(arrives | departed) = $\frac{0.78}{0.83} = 0.94$
  • P(departed | arrived) = $\frac{0.78}{0.82} = 0.95$

Independent Events

Events A and B are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the other:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad P(B|A) = P(B)$$

Example: Fire engine available P(A)=0.98, ambulance available P(B)=0.92, operating independently. P(both available) = $0.98 \times 0.92 = 0.9016$.

Bayes' Theorem

Used to "reverse" conditional probabilities — find P(cause | effect) from P(effect | cause). This is the foundation of Bayesian estimation.

$$P(A_i | B) = \frac{P(A_i) \cdot P(B|A_i)}{\sum_j P(A_j) \cdot P(B|A_j)}$$

Where $A_1, A_2, \dots$ form a partition of the sample space (mutually exclusive & exhaustive). The denominator is the total probability $P(B)$.

Total Probability Theorem: $P(B) = \sum_j P(A_j) P(B|A_j)$

Why this matters for the exam: Basic probability rarely appears as a standalone question in the ETE (it's "only one lecture"), but it is embedded in nearly every distribution and estimation problem. Conditional probability + independence is essential for the exponential distribution's memoryless property and for joint/independent random variables.

Key Concepts & Theory

Foundational Terms (Often asked in Section A)

Population & Parameter
The population is the entire group under study. A parameter is a numerical characteristic of the population (e.g., population mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$). Parameters are usually unknown and fixed.
Sample & Statistic
A sample is a subset of the population. A statistic is a numerical characteristic computed from the sample (e.g., sample mean $\bar{x}$, sample variance $s^2$). A statistic is used to estimate a parameter and varies from sample to sample.
Standard Error (SE)
The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic. It measures how precisely a statistic estimates the parameter. For the sample mean: $SE(\bar{x}) = \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ (or $\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$ when $\sigma$ is unknown). Smaller SE = more reliable estimate. Content rephrased from referenced sources for compliance.
Sampling Distribution
The probability distribution of a statistic obtained from all possible samples of a given size. The test statistics (t, F, $\chi^2$) follow known sampling distributions.

Definitions (Section A - Memorize)

Statistical Hypothesis
An assertion or conjecture about the distribution of one or more random variables (population parameters).
Null Hypothesis ($H_0$)
The hypothesis being tested - typically states "no effect" or "no difference". We assume it to be true unless evidence suggests otherwise.
Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$)
The hypothesis accepted when $H_0$ is rejected. Can be one-tailed ($\mu > \mu_0$ or $\mu < \mu_0$) or two-tailed ($\mu \neq \mu_0$).
Type I Error ($\alpha$)
Rejecting $H_0$ when it is actually TRUE. Probability = level of significance.
Type II Error ($\beta$)
Accepting (failing to reject) $H_0$ when it is actually FALSE. The power of a test = $1 - \beta$.
Critical Region (Rejection Region)
The set of values of the test statistic that leads to rejection of $H_0$. Its boundary is the critical value.
Level of Significance ($\alpha$)
The maximum probability of Type I error we are willing to tolerate (usually 0.05 or 0.01).
Confidence Interval & Limits
A range of values within which the true parameter is likely to lie. Limits are the endpoints of this interval.

The 6-Step Testing Procedure (use for EVERY test)

  1. State hypotheses: $H_0$ and $H_1$ (decide one- or two-tailed).
  2. Set level of significance $\alpha$ (given, usually 0.05 or 0.01).
  3. Compute the test statistic (t, F, or $\chi^2$) using the right formula.
  4. Find the critical/tabulated value from tables using $\alpha$ and degrees of freedom.
  5. Decision rule: Reject $H_0$ if |calculated| > tabulated (else do not reject).
  6. Conclusion stated in the context of the problem.

t-test for Single Mean

When to use: Testing if a population mean equals a specified value when population variance is unknown and sample is small (n < 30).

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}, \quad df = n-1$$

Decision Rule:

  • Two-tailed: Reject $H_0$ if $|t_{calc}| > t_{\alpha/2, n-1}$
  • One-tailed (right): Reject $H_0$ if $t_{calc} > t_{\alpha, n-1}$
  • One-tailed (left): Reject $H_0$ if $t_{calc} < -t_{\alpha, n-1}$

t-test for Two Independent Means

When to use: Comparing means of two independent groups (assuming equal variances).

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}, \quad df = n_1 + n_2 - 2$$

Where pooled standard deviation: $s_p = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}$

Paired t-test (Dependent Samples)

When to use: When observations are paired (before/after, husband/wife, left/right hand).

$$t = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d / \sqrt{n}}, \quad df = n-1$$

Where $\bar{d}$ = mean of differences, $s_d$ = standard deviation of differences, $n$ = number of pairs.

F-test (Test of Variances)

When to use: Comparing variances of two populations.

$$F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2} \quad (s_1^2 > s_2^2), \quad df_1 = n_1-1, \quad df_2 = n_2-1$$

Rule: Always put larger variance in numerator. Reject $H_0$ if $F_{calc} > F_{\alpha, df_1, df_2}$.

Chi-Square Test (Independence of Attributes)

When to use: Testing if two categorical variables are independent.

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}, \quad E_i = \frac{(\text{Row Total}) \times (\text{Column Total})}{\text{Grand Total}}$$

$df = (r-1)(c-1)$ where r = rows, c = columns. Reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2_{calc} > \chi^2_{\alpha, df}$.

One-Way ANOVA

When to use: Comparing means of 3 or more groups simultaneously.

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = ... = \mu_k$ vs $H_1:$ At least one mean differs

$$F = \frac{MSB}{MSW} = \frac{SSB/(k-1)}{SSW/(N-k)}$$

Key Concepts & Theory

Binomial Distribution

When: Fixed number of trials (n), each with same probability (p), independent, two outcomes.

$$P(X=x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x}, \quad x = 0, 1, 2, ..., n$$

Mean: $E(X) = np$ | Variance: $Var(X) = npq$ where $q = 1-p$

Key Properties:

  • $Mean > Variance$ always (since $q < 1$)
  • If mean = variance, it's NOT binomial
  • Sum of two Binomial RVs with same p is also Binomial

Poisson Distribution

When: Counting rare events in a fixed interval (time/area/volume). n large, p small, $\lambda = np$ moderate.

$$P(X=x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^x}{x!}, \quad x = 0, 1, 2, ...$$

Mean = Variance = $\lambda$

Key Properties:

  • Mean = Variance (unique identifying property)
  • Sum of independent Poisson RVs is Poisson with $\lambda = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$
  • If $P(X=k) = P(X=k+1)$, then $\lambda = k+1$

Uniform Distribution

When: All outcomes equally likely in an interval [a, b].

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}, \quad a \leq x \leq b$$

Mean: $\frac{a+b}{2}$ | Variance: $\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}$

CDF: $F(x) = \frac{x-a}{b-a}$

Normal Distribution

When: Continuous data that is symmetric, bell-shaped.

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \quad -\infty < x < \infty$$

Standard Normal: $Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$, then $Z \sim N(0,1)$

Key Properties:

  • 68% within $\pm 1\sigma$, 95% within $\pm 2\sigma$, 99.7% within $\pm 3\sigma$
  • Mean = Median = Mode
  • Sum of normal RVs is also normal

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Statement: If $X_1, X_2, ..., X_n$ are i.i.d. random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then as $n \to \infty$:

$$\bar{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \quad \text{or} \quad Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)$$

Key Point: Works regardless of the original distribution (for $n \geq 30$).

Application: Used to find probability that sample mean lies in a given range.

🧮 Which Distribution to Use? (Recognition Guide)

Clue in the problemDistribution
Fixed n trials, "success/failure", probability p (e.g. defective screws, boys/girls)Binomial
Rare events / counts per interval; "average number of...", errors per page, calls per hourPoisson
"Equally likely" over an interval; waiting time within fixed period; random point in [a,b]Uniform
Bell-shaped, "mean and SD given", heights/marks/rainfall, large-sample approximationsNormal
Time until an event, lifetime, time between arrivals; "memoryless"Exponential

Handy links: Poisson approximates Binomial when n large, p small ($\lambda = np$). Normal approximates Binomial when n large ($\mu = np$, $\sigma^2 = npq$).

Key Concepts & Theory

Exponential Distribution

$$f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad x \geq 0$$

Mean: $1/\lambda$ | Variance: $1/\lambda^2$ | CDF: $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$

Memoryless Property: $P(X > s+t | X > s) = P(X > t)$ - unique to exponential among continuous distributions.

Survival Function: $P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$

Properties of a Good Estimator

Unbiasedness
$E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$. The expected value of the estimator equals the true parameter. E.g., sample mean $\bar{x}$ is unbiased for $\mu$; sample variance $s^2$ (with $n-1$ divisor) is unbiased for $\sigma^2$.
Consistency
$\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{P} \theta$ as $n \to \infty$. As sample size grows, the estimator converges to the true value (bias and variance both shrink to 0).
Efficiency
Among all unbiased estimators, the one with the minimum variance is most efficient (MVUE = Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimator). Efficiency = ratio of minimum possible variance (Cramer-Rao bound) to the estimator's variance.
Sufficiency
A statistic $T(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if it captures ALL information about $\theta$ contained in the sample — no other statistic can add information about $\theta$.

Memory aid: U-C-E-S (Unbiased, Consistent, Efficient, Sufficient). An ideal estimator has all four.

Sufficient Statistics (Fisher-Neyman Factorization)

Theorem: A statistic $T(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if and only if:

$$f(x|\theta) = g(T(x), \theta) \cdot h(x)$$

Where $g$ depends on data only through $T(x)$, and $h$ is independent of $\theta$.

Common Sufficient Statistics:

  • Normal($\mu$, $\sigma^2$ known): $\sum x_i$ (or $\bar{x}$) is sufficient for $\mu$
  • Poisson($\lambda$): $\sum x_i$ is sufficient for $\lambda$
  • Exponential($\lambda$): $\sum x_i$ is sufficient for $\lambda$
  • Binomial(n, p): $\sum x_i$ is sufficient for p

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Method:

  1. Write the likelihood function: $L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i | \theta)$
  2. Take log-likelihood: $\ln L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln f(x_i | \theta)$
  3. Differentiate and set to zero: $\frac{d}{d\theta} \ln L = 0$
  4. Solve for $\hat{\theta}_{MLE}$
  5. Verify it's a maximum (second derivative < 0)

Common MLEs:

  • Normal($\mu$, $\sigma^2$): $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$, $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2$
  • Exponential($\lambda$): $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{x}$
  • Poisson($\lambda$): $\hat{\lambda} = \bar{x}$
  • Bernoulli(p): $\hat{p} = \bar{x}$
  • Uniform(0, $\theta$): $\hat{\theta} = \max(x_1, ..., x_n)$

Worked Example: MLE for Exponential Distribution

Given a random sample $x_1, \dots, x_n$ from $f(x;\lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, find $\hat{\lambda}_{MLE}$.

Step 1 — Likelihood: $L(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda e^{-\lambda x_i} = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i}$

Step 2 — Log-likelihood: $\ln L = n\ln\lambda - \lambda\sum x_i$

Step 3 — Differentiate & set to 0: $\dfrac{d\ln L}{d\lambda} = \dfrac{n}{\lambda} - \sum x_i = 0$

Step 4 — Solve: $\hat{\lambda} = \dfrac{n}{\sum x_i} = \dfrac{1}{\bar{x}}$

Step 5 — Verify max: $\dfrac{d^2\ln L}{d\lambda^2} = -\dfrac{n}{\lambda^2} < 0$ ✓ (it is a maximum).

Worked Example: Method of Moments for Uniform(0, θ)

For $U(0,\theta)$, the population mean is $E(X) = \theta/2$.

Equate to sample mean: $\dfrac{\theta}{2} = \bar{x} \;\Rightarrow\; \hat{\theta}_{MoM} = 2\bar{x}$.

Note: Compare with MLE $\hat{\theta} = \max(x_i)$ — different methods can give different estimators for the same parameter!

MLE vs MoM — which is "better"? MLE is generally more efficient (smaller variance) and is consistent & asymptotically normal, but can be harder to compute. MoM is simpler (just equate moments) but can be less efficient. Both are consistent. Questions often ask you to derive BOTH for the same distribution and compare.

Method of Moments (MoM)

Method:

  1. Express population moments in terms of parameters: $E(X) = g_1(\theta)$, $E(X^2) = g_2(\theta)$, ...
  2. Equate population moments to sample moments: $\bar{x} = g_1(\hat{\theta})$, $\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^2 = g_2(\hat{\theta})$
  3. Solve for parameters

Sample Moments:

  • First moment: $m_1 = \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$
  • Second moment: $m_2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^2$
  • Variance: $m_2 - m_1^2$

Common MoM Estimators:

  • Normal: $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$, $\hat{\sigma}^2 = m_2 - \bar{x}^2$
  • Exponential: $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{x}$
  • Uniform[a,b]: $\hat{a} = \bar{x} - \sqrt{3s^2}$, $\hat{b} = \bar{x} + \sqrt{3s^2}$

Key Concepts & Theory

Random Variable

A function that assigns a real number to each outcome in a sample space. Can be discrete (countable values) or continuous (uncountable/interval values).

Example: Height of persons = continuous RV. Number on a die = discrete RV.

Probability Mass Function (PMF) - Discrete

$P(X = x_i) = p(x_i)$ where:

  • $p(x_i) \geq 0$ for all $x_i$
  • $\sum_i p(x_i) = 1$

Probability Density Function (PDF) - Continuous

$f(x)$ where:

  • $f(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$
  • $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) dx = 1$
  • $P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b f(x) dx$

Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

$$F(x) = P(X \leq x) = \begin{cases} \sum_{x_i \leq x} p(x_i) & \text{(discrete)} \\ \int_{-\infty}^x f(t) dt & \text{(continuous)} \end{cases}$$

Properties:

  • $0 \leq F(x) \leq 1$
  • $F(-\infty) = 0$, $F(\infty) = 1$
  • Non-decreasing function
  • $f(x) = F'(x)$ (for continuous)

Expectation & Variance

$$E(X) = \begin{cases} \sum x_i p(x_i) & \text{(discrete)} \\ \int x f(x) dx & \text{(continuous)} \end{cases}$$
$$Var(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2$$

Properties:

  • $E(aX + b) = aE(X) + b$
  • $Var(aX + b) = a^2 Var(X)$
  • If X, Y independent: $E(XY) = E(X) \cdot E(Y)$
  • $Var(X \pm Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)$ (if independent)

Independent Random Variables

Two random variables X and Y are independent if their joint distribution factorizes:

$$f(x,y) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y(y) \quad\text{(continuous)}, \qquad P(X=x, Y=y) = P(X=x)P(Y=y)\ \text{(discrete)}$$

Consequences for independent X, Y:

  • $E(XY) = E(X)E(Y)$
  • $Var(aX \pm bY) = a^2 Var(X) + b^2 Var(Y)$  (note: it's always + on the right)
  • $Cov(X,Y) = 0$ (independence implies zero covariance; converse not always true)

This is why, in the MTE Poisson problem, $Var(X-2Y) = Var(X) + 4Var(Y)$ — the minus sign becomes plus and the constant gets squared.

Chebyshev's Inequality

Statement: For any random variable X with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$:

$$P(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}$$

Equivalent forms:

  • $P(|X - \mu| < k\sigma) \geq 1 - \frac{1}{k^2}$
  • $P(|X - \mu| \geq k) \leq \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2}$

Key Points:

  • Works for ANY distribution (no normality required)
  • Gives a conservative (loose) bound
  • Often asked: compute bound AND actual probability, then compare

Key Concepts & Theory

Bayesian Estimation

Key Idea: Treats parameters as random variables with prior distributions. Combines prior knowledge with data to get posterior distribution.

$$\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}$$ $$f(\theta|x) = \frac{f(x|\theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)}{\int f(x|\theta) \cdot \pi(\theta) \, d\theta}$$

Bayes Estimator: The mean of the posterior distribution (under squared error loss).

Steps:

  1. Choose a prior distribution $\pi(\theta)$ for the parameter
  2. Write the likelihood $f(x|\theta)$ from the data
  3. Compute posterior $f(\theta|x) \propto f(x|\theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)$
  4. Find the Bayes estimate = $E(\theta|x)$ = mean of posterior

Intuition: The 3-Coins Example (from material PDF)

Suppose someone carries 3 coins: one 2-headed (p=1), one 2-tailed (p=0), one fair (p=0.5). They pick one at random (prior = 1/3 each), flip 3 times, and get 2 heads (n=3, x=2).

  • MLE/MoM would ignore the prior and estimate $\hat{p} = 2/3$ — but that's impossible since only p = 0, 0.5, 1 exist!
  • Bayesian uses priors. Likelihood $P(X{=}2|p) = \binom{3}{2}p^2(1-p)$: for p=1 → 0, p=0 → 0, p=0.5 → 0.375.
  • Posterior $\propto$ prior × likelihood. Only the fair coin gives non-zero posterior → conclude it's the fair coin (p = 0.5).

Key takeaway: Bayesian estimation incorporates prior knowledge, while MLE and MoM rely only on the observed sample. Content rephrased from material for compliance.

Prior → Posterior: The posterior probability is proportional to (prior × likelihood). The parameter value with the highest posterior is the best Bayesian estimate. For continuous parameters, the Bayes estimate (under squared-error loss) is the mean of the posterior distribution.

Confidence Intervals for Means

Large Sample (n ≥ 30) - Z-interval

$$\bar{x} \pm Z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

If $\sigma$ unknown, use $s$ (sample SD). Common Z-values: $Z_{0.025} = 1.96$ (95%), $Z_{0.005} = 2.576$ (99%)

Small Sample (n < 30) - t-interval

$$\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Use t-distribution with $df = n-1$.

Difference of Two Means

$$(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2, n_1+n_2-2} \cdot s_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}$$

Q: Given $f(x,\theta) = 1/\theta$, $0 \leq x \leq \theta$ and 0 otherwise. Testing $H_0: \theta=1$ against $H_1: \theta=2$ using single observed value. Find Type I and Type II errors for critical regions (i) $0.5 \leq x$ (ii) $1 \leq x \leq 1.5$.

Solution

(i) CR: $x \geq 0.5$

$\alpha = P(\text{reject } H_0 | H_0 \text{ true}) = P(X \geq 0.5 | \theta=1) = \int_{0.5}^1 1 \, dx = 0.5$

$\beta = P(\text{accept } H_0 | H_1 \text{ true}) = P(X < 0.5 | \theta=2) = \int_0^{0.5} (1/2) \, dx = 0.25$

(ii) CR: $1 \leq x \leq 1.5$

$\alpha = P(1 \leq X \leq 1.5 | \theta=1) = 0$ (X can't exceed 1 when $\theta=1$)

$\beta = P(X \notin [1,1.5] | \theta=2) = P(X<1 | \theta=2) + P(X>1.5 | \theta=2) = 0.5 + 0.25 = 0.75$

$= 1 - [405/1024 + 243/1024] = 1 - 648/1024 = 376/1024... $

Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls

Easy marks are lost to these recurring errors. Skim this list the morning of the exam.

✗
Using n instead of n−1 for sample variance in a t-test. ✓ Sample SD uses divisor (n−1).
✗
Putting the smaller variance on top in the F-test. ✓ Always larger variance in the numerator so F ≥ 1.
✗
Confusing one-tailed vs two-tailed critical values. ✓ "less than / greater than" = one-tailed; "differs / not equal" = two-tailed (use α/2).
✗
Wrong degrees of freedom. ✓ 1-sample t: n−1; 2-sample t: n₁+n₂−2; Chi-sq: (r−1)(c−1); ANOVA: k−1 & N−k.
✗
For Poisson, forgetting mean = variance = λ. ✓ If mean ≠ variance, it's not Poisson.
✗
Mixing Z-table conventions. ✓ Our table gives P(0<Z<z). For P(Z>z) do 0.5−value.
✗
For Var(aX − bY) writing a minus sign. ✓ Variances always add: a²Var(X)+b²Var(Y) when independent.
✗
Swapping Type I and Type II errors. ✓ Type I (α) = reject a TRUE H₀; Type II (β) = accept a FALSE H₀.
✗
Forgetting expected frequency formula in Chi-square. ✓ E = (row total × column total) / grand total.
✗
Not stating a conclusion in words. ✓ Always finish with "reject/accept H₀, so ... is/ is not significant."
✗
Exponential units: using rate as mean. ✓ Mean = 1/λ. If "mean 6 min", then λ=1/6.
✗
MLE: differentiating L instead of ln L. ✓ Always take log first; it's far easier and gives the same maximizer.

$\ln L = n\ln\lambda - \lambda\sum x_i$; set derivative to 0: $n/\lambda - \sum x_i = 0 \Rightarrow \hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{x}$.

Type I (α): rejecting H₀ when it is true. Type II (β): accepting H₀ when it is false. Power = 1−β.

(r−1)(c−1) = (3−1)(3−1) = 4.

Distributions (mean / variance)

  • Binomial: np / npq
  • Poisson: λ / λ (mean = var)
  • Uniform(a,b): (a+b)/2 / (b−a)²/12
  • Normal: μ / σ²;   Z = (X−μ)/σ
  • Exponential(λ): 1/λ / 1/λ²;   P(X>x)=e^(−λx)

Hypothesis Tests

  • 1-sample t: (x̄−μ₀)/(s/√n), df=n−1
  • 2-sample t: (x̄₁−x̄₂)/(s_p√(1/n₁+1/n₂)), df=n₁+n₂−2
  • Paired t: d̄/(s_d/√n), df=n−1
  • F: s₁²/s₂² (larger top), df=(n₁−1,n₂−1)
  • Chi-sq: ∑(O−E)²/E, E=row×col/total, df=(r−1)(c−1)
  • ANOVA: F=MSB/MSW, df=(k−1, N−k)

Estimation

  • MLE: max of ln L → d(ln L)/dθ=0
  • Exp: λ̂=1/x̄ · Poisson: λ̂=x̄ · Normal: μ̂=x̄
  • MoM: equate E(X^k) to (1/n)∑x_i^k
  • Bayes: posterior ∝ prior × likelihood
  • Good estimator: U-C-E-S

Key Constants

  • Z: 90%→1.645, 95%→1.96, 99%→2.576
  • χ²(df=1): 3.841 · (df=4): 9.488
  • t(9): 2-tail .05 = 2.262, 1-tail = 1.833
  • Chebyshev: P(|X−μ|≥kσ) ≤ 1/k²
  • CLT: x̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n)

Confidence Intervals

  • Large n: x̄ ± Z_(α/2)·σ/√n
  • Small n: x̄ ± t_(α/2,n−1)·s/√n

Errors

  • Type I (α): reject TRUE H₀
  • Type II (β): accept FALSE H₀
  • Power = 1 − β

6-Step Test Procedure

  1. State H₀ & H₁
  2. Set α
  3. Compute test statistic
  4. Find critical value (table)
  5. Decide: |calc| > crit → reject
  6. Conclude in words

Estimation

  • MLE: $\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln L(\theta) = 0$
  • MoM: Equate $E(X^r) = m_r = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^r$
  • Sufficiency: $f(x|\theta) = g(T(x),\theta) \cdot h(x)$
  • Bayes: Posterior $\propto$ Likelihood $\times$ Prior

Confidence Intervals

  • Large sample: $\bar{x} \pm Z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$
  • Small sample: $\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,n-1} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$
  • Z-values: 90%: 1.645, 95%: 1.96, 99%: 2.576

Key Inequalities & Theorems

  • Chebyshev: $P(|X-\mu| \geq k\sigma) \leq 1/k^2$
  • CLT: $\bar{X} \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ for large n
  • Memoryless: $P(X>s+t|X>s) = P(X>t)$ (Exponential only)

Common MLEs

  • Normal($\mu$): $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$
  • Normal($\sigma^2$): $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2$
  • Exponential($\lambda$): $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{x}$
  • Poisson($\lambda$): $\hat{\lambda} = \bar{x}$
  • Uniform(0,$\theta$): $\hat{\theta} = \max(x_i)$
  • Bernoulli(p): $\hat{p} = \bar{x}$
